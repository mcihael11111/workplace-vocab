## How both books write, and what your product can learn from them

### Laws of UX: the format

Every law follows the same tight structure:
1. **A single declarative sentence** that names the law ("The time to acquire a target is a function of the distance to and size of the target.")
2. **A brief origin paragraph** grounding it in psychology
3. **One or two concrete examples** showing how it applies
4. **Key takeaways** as scannable bullet points

The writing never hedges. It states the principle, shows why it matters, and moves on. There is no preamble, no "in the world of design..." warm-up. The reader gets the concept in under two minutes.

### 100 Things: the format

Each entry is 1-2 pages. It opens with a **relatable story or surprising fact** that hooks the reader before the principle is named. The psychology comes after the hook, not before. Every entry ends with a boxed **Takeaways** section: 3-5 bullet points of direct, actionable advice.

Weinschenk's secret weapon is that she leads with recognition ("You've experienced this...") rather than instruction ("This concept means..."). The reader feels smart before they learn something new.

## 1. Lead definitions with a plain sentence, not a technical one

### The principle (from both books)

Yablonski opens every law with one clean sentence a non-expert can repeat back. Weinschenk opens every entry with a moment of recognition. Your definitions often open with a technically precise sentence that reads like a reference manual.

The fix: **first sentence should be what it is in plain language. Second sentence adds precision.**

### Examples of changed copy

**Affinity Map**

> **Current:** "A method for organising large amounts of qualitative data (sticky notes, observations, quotes) into themed clusters that reveal patterns and shared meaning across a research set."

> **Revised:** "A way of sorting messy research into groups so patterns become obvious. Teams take raw observations, quotes, and notes, then cluster them by theme until shared meaning emerges across the data."

Why this works: The first sentence is repeatable. Someone could leave the card and explain it to a colleague. The technical precision (qualitative data, themed clusters) comes second, for those who want it.

---

**Cognitive Load**

> **Current:** "The total mental effort a person uses to process and act on information at any given moment. When a design introduces too many decisions, unclear labels, or competing visual elements at once, working memory gets overloaded and users slow down, make mistakes, or leave."

> **Revised:** "How much mental effort someone is spending to understand what's in front of them. When a screen asks for too many decisions at once, working memory overloads and people slow down, make mistakes, or leave."

Why this works: "Total mental effort a person uses to process and act on information at any given moment" is 17 words doing the job of 10. The revised version says the same thing in plain language first.

---

**Customer Journey Map**

> **Current:** "A visual representation of the steps a user takes when interacting with a product or service, from first awareness through to completion. It captures emotions, touchpoints, and friction at each stage of the experience."

> **Revised:** "A visual timeline of what someone goes through when using a product, from first hearing about it to finishing the task. It makes emotions, friction, and drop-off points visible at each stage."

---

**Heuristic Evaluation**

> **Current:** "A usability inspection method where experts evaluate an interface against a set of established usability principles, most commonly Jakob Nielsen's 10 heuristics, to identify violations without involving real users."

> **Revised:** "A structured review where experienced designers walk through an interface checking it against established usability principles. The most common framework is Jakob Nielsen's 10 heuristics, and the goal is catching problems before users ever see the product."

Why this works: Breaking the single dense sentence into two lets the reader absorb the concept before the specifics. The parenthetical reference to Nielsen moves into its own sentence where it can breathe.

---

**Persona**

> **Current:** "A fictional but research-grounded character representing a key segment of your users. A persona captures goals, behaviours, frustrations, and context rather than demographics alone, and gives the team a shared reference point when making decisions."

> **Revised:** "A fictional character built from real research that represents a key group of your users. Instead of just demographics, a persona captures goals, frustrations, and context, giving the team a shared reference point when decisions get contentious."

## 3. Shorten "Why It Matters" sections that restate the definition

### The principle (from Yablonski)

Laws of UX never explains a concept twice. The "why" section adds a new dimension, usually tension or consequence. Several of your whyItMatters sections spend their first sentence restating what the definition already said.

### Examples of changed copy

**Contrast:**

> **Current whyItMatters:** "Low contrast is hard to read for people with visual impairments but also for everyone on a bright screen, a low-quality display, or in sunlight. WCAG requires a minimum 4.5:1 ratio for normal text, and meeting it benefits every user."

> **Revised:** "Low contrast is not just an accessibility issue. Everyone struggles on bright screens, cheap displays, and outdoor light. WCAG requires a 4.5:1 ratio for normal text. Meeting it benefits every user, not just the ones who need it."

Why this works: Opens with a reframe ("not just an accessibility issue") rather than restating the definition. Creates an aha moment.

---

**Colour Palette:**

> **Current whyItMatters:** "A well-defined palette creates visual cohesion and makes colour decisions faster. Without one, teams make choices in isolation and the product accumulates inconsistencies that erode trust over time."

> **Revised:** "Without a defined palette, every designer picks their own shade of blue. Over time the product accumulates dozens of near-identical colours that collectively make it feel unpolished. A palette is not about aesthetics. It is about preventing drift."

Why this works: Opens with a concrete image ("every designer picks their own shade of blue") rather than an abstract benefit. Follows the Weinschenk pattern of recognition before instruction.

---

**Call to Action:**

> **Current whyItMatters:** "A page without a clear CTA is a page without a purpose. The clarity, placement, and visual weight of a CTA directly affects conversion. If users do not know what to do next, they will do nothing."

> **Revised:** "A page without a clear CTA is a page without a purpose. If people do not know what to do next, they will do nothing. Clarity, placement, and visual weight determine whether someone clicks or leaves."

Why this works: Moves "if users don't know what to do next" right after the opening line for stronger cause-and-effect. Drops the abstract "directly affects conversion" for the concrete "clicks or leaves."

## 5. Tighten psychology sections

### The principle (from both books)

Both books name the principle in a single sentence, then connect it to the topic in one more. Your psychology sections often spend 2-3 sentences explaining the principle before connecting it. Trim the explanation; trust the reader.

### Examples of changed copy

**Affinity Map psychology:**

> **Current:** "Without synthesis, each team member unconsciously prioritises observations that resonated with them personally, a phenomenon driven by Confirmation Bias. Affinity mapping counteracts this by forcing the group to cluster raw data before interpreting it, reducing the chance that any one person's bias shapes the conclusion."

> **Revised:** "Confirmation Bias leads each team member to unconsciously prioritise observations that resonated with them personally. Affinity mapping counteracts this by forcing the group to cluster raw data before interpreting it, so no one person's bias shapes the conclusion."

Why this works: Names the principle first (Confirmation Bias), then connects it. The reader knows what to anchor on.

---

**Persona psychology:**

> **Current:** "Personas combat the False Consensus Effect, the tendency to assume others think and behave the way we do. Without a shared reference character grounded in research, every team member unconsciously designs for themselves. A well-built persona externalises the user so the team can stop debating preferences and start referencing evidence."

> **Revised:** "The False Consensus Effect is the tendency to assume others think the way we do. Personas counteract it by externalising the user into a shared reference grounded in research, so the team debates evidence instead of personal preferences."

Why this works: Three sentences become two. Same information, less processing.

---

**Toggle psychology:**

> **Current:** "Toggles feel intuitive because they mimic a physical switch, leveraging the brain's familiarity with real-world objects. However, ambiguity about the current state triggers a subtle cognitive conflict: the user cannot tell if the label describes what will happen or what is already happening. Resolving this ambiguity is critical because uncertainty, even minor uncertainty, erodes the user's sense of control."

> **Revised:** "Toggles feel intuitive because they mimic physical switches the brain already understands. But if the label does not make the current state clear, the user cannot tell whether it describes what will happen or what is already happening. Even minor ambiguity erodes the sense of control."

## 6. Break up dense paragraphs in the About page

### The principle (from Weinschenk)

Weinschenk's research shows people read in F-patterns and scan before committing. Your "Who it's for" section is a single dense paragraph listing four audience types. Breaking it into distinct lines makes each persona scannable.

### Current copy

### Revised copy

>

Why this works: Each persona gets its own line. The reader finds themselves faster. Removes "Or" and "Maybe" connectors that slow scanning. Follows both books' principle that **one idea per visual block** is easier to process.

## 10. Use the Weinschenk "Takeaways" pattern for the psychology sections

### The recommendation

This is not about changing existing copy but about a structural improvement inspired directly by Weinschenk. Each psychology section could end with a single-line, bold takeaway that names the design implication. This gives the reader something actionable to remember.

### Example format

**Current (Cognitive Load psychology):**
> "Cognitive Load Theory identifies three types of load: intrinsic (task complexity), extraneous (poor design adding unnecessary effort), and germane (effort that aids learning). Good design minimises extraneous load so working memory is free for the task itself."

**Revised with takeaway:**
> "Cognitive Load Theory identifies three types of load: intrinsic (task complexity), extraneous (poor design), and germane (effort that aids learning). Good design minimises extraneous load so working memory is free for the task itself."
>
> **Design implication:** If the user is struggling, remove distractions before simplifying the task.

**Current (Hick's Law, from CTA psychology):**
> "Hick's Law states that the time to make a decision increases logarithmically with the number of choices."
>
> **Design implication:** One clear action outperforms a cluster of options.

This mirrors exactly how Yablonski closes each law chapter: a concrete, quotable line the reader can carry into their next design review.

## The thread that connects both books

Yablonski and Weinschenk write differently, but they share the same belief: **respect the reader's working memory.** Every formatting choice they make, from Yablonski's single-sentence law statements to Weinschenk's boxed takeaways, exists to reduce the effort between "I opened this" and "I understand it."

Your product already embodies this philosophy in its tone. The changes above bring the *structure* of the copy in line with the same principle: make each section do one job, lead with recognition, and get out of the way.
